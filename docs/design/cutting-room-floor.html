Before the first cut, the floor.

Option B gives the editing a room. This is the door: open a folder → index it, unattended and resumable → walk the cutting room floor → fill the bin → assemble. The intake is where an AI-first editor earns its name — not by cutting for you, but by watching everything so you don't have to, showing what it found as a film rather than a list, and letting you keep or reject at the speed of a photo cull. This version incorporates an independent review by a video-editing UI designer (§9) and four decisions from Karl (§0).

§0Decided

Four answers that shaped everything below

Keep = ?What you actually watchedThe kept range is the extent you saw — the preview, or more if you held to keep watching — snapped outward to sentence ends. Extending past that is a deliberate key. Windows stay honest; the bin's length means something.
KeysP / X / U, JKL intactPick, reject, unflag-as-later; 1 for hero. Shuttle is J-K-L exactly as in every NLE — K is pause, never a verdict. Auto-advance is a Caps Lock toggle, Lightroom's pattern.
Index cadenceUnattended, resumable, priority-ordered, released per clipNot "edit after four minutes." The index runs on its own with progress tracked and survives a crash. Clips are indexed in a priority order and handed to the floor fully indexed, one by one, while the rest continues.
Footage cadenceOnce at home — but clips may be added at any timeOne project for the trip; a friend's clip or a forgotten file can arrive later, several at once. Appending is a first-class path, robust by construction.

Two consequences fall out immediately. The trailer is gone — it existed to give you something to watch at minute four, which is no longer a goal, and "play the bin" does its sanity-check job better and for free. And the index is a journal, not a job: per-clip stages on disk, a priority queue, and a resume path, so both "it crashed" and "I added three clips" are the same operation — index what isn't done.

Three things distinguish this from the current in-app sequence (Analyse audio → Look at the footage → Ask for a first cut). Listening comes before deciding what to look for — the transcripts alone found the spine of every bin so far. And selection is a first-class artifact: the bin is a real thing in the EDL, anchored to clip time, that survives re-indexing and added footage.

§2Stages 1–3

Open: know the bin before you pay for it

The open screen is a contact sheet of the folder — first frame, duration, capture time grouped into sessions (the 4-hour-gap rule already in shot_timeline), and the free flags: junk (the conservative luma<11 band), rotation side-data (per bin, confirmed once), telemetry present. That is the roadmap's "junk and orientation proposed, human confirms," done at the door.

The review was right that the first draft's four-tier granularity ladder was a form, and non-monotonic. It is now one slider — sample interval, 4 s to 1 s, with price and clock reading out live and the default detent at coarse-plus-close-look. Workers is a setting in a drawer (it is a machine property, not a per-run decision), defaulting to the measured sweet spot once the request-rate study runs. Closer looks are bought per pick on the floor, at $0.07 each, where they're worth it.

Adding footage is the same screen: drop files into the folder or press Add clips. New stems are detected against the journal, listened to immediately, and queued for indexing behind nothing; their picks reach the floor as a new material round. Existing verdicts, notes and keeps are untouched, because they are anchored to clip time, not to pick numbers.

§3Stage 4

Index: unattended, resumable, released per clip

Karl's instruction, verbatim in spirit: the index should run on its own with progress tracked, survive a crash, work through the clips in a priority order, and hand each clip to the floor the moment it is fully indexed — while the rest continues. Not "start editing at minute four," which is tempting for iterating on the app and wrong for the person using it. The design is therefore a journal and a queue:

  • The journal is one file per project under --work: for every clip, the state of each stage — probe, telemetry, ASR, proxy, look, close look, picks — as done / running / failed / queued, with attempts and cost, plus the settings the run was made with. The sidecars on disk stay the source of truth (the pipeline already counts progress from them); the journal is the plan. On restart the two are reconciled and every stage that isn't done is re-queued. A crash mid-sheet costs one sheet; nothing already paid for is re-bought.
  • The priority order comes from the free stages. Once every clip is listened to, each gets a score — speech candidates, interest markers, telemetry peaks, theme hits if themes were given, with a small weight for duration — and the priced stages run in that order. The clips most likely to carry the film reach the floor first; ties fall back to capture order. It's a default: capture order is one click away for a chronological cull.
  • Release is per clip and all-or-nothing. A clip appears on the floor only when its proxy, sheets, close look and picks all exist. The floor never shows a clip whose picture can't play or whose picks might still change. The remaining clips keep indexing behind it, with the same bar and the same cost meter.
  • Added footage is the same path. New stems not in the journal go through the free stages immediately and join the queue; nothing about existing clips re-runs. Removed or renamed files are matched by duration and content hash; a keep whose footage is missing is flagged, never deleted.
  • Failure is a state, not a stop. A rate-limited sheet backs off and retries; a clip that fails a stage three times is parked with the reason and the rest continues; hitting the budget cap pauses the priced stages only, and the floor opens on what's released.

§4Stage 5 — where the time goes

The pass: cull, don't browse

The mental model is photo culling: Lightroom made a thousand frames an hour's work by giving every frame the same keys and always showing the picture big. The pass does that with picks — windows the index proposed, each with a reason and its witnesses — played large, judged on one key, trimmed by what you watched, annotated by voice. This is the third draft of the screen; the second was evaluated by an independent reviewer (§9) and its faults are this draft's spec.

4.1 One pick, in about seven seconds

The picture fills the centre and starts on the moment. The pillarbox margins that a 16:9 picture leaves anyway carry the chrome: the clip's context on the left, the witnesses on the right — nothing is painted on the picture except the verdict stamp. Below the picture, the machine's reason in one line, generated only from the witnesses that agree; when they disagree, the line says so. Under that, the two-tier strip: the whole clip as a tape with every pick marked, and a zoomed strip that follows the playhead. You press P — or hold V and say why first — a stamp lands, and the next pick begins.

4.3 Rounds, order, and long films

Cull time is bounded by the target, not the footage. Picks are taken from released clips, 40 to a round, and the queue is frozen for the round. A round ends with a card that says what the bin holds and what arrived meanwhile. You stop when you have the moments you need, not when the footage runs out; the footage past that is where the machine's ranking earns its keep by never showing it to you.

  • Preview rule. A pick under ~8 s plays whole. A longer one plays the moment ±3 s; hold space to keep watching. What you watch is what you keep.
  • Order. Rank (default) or by clip (chronological, guarantees coverage — Resolve's source-tape idea). By-session is by-clip with headers; by-theme is a filter on rank. Two modes at launch.
  • Batch verdicts. ⇧X rejects the rest of this clip's picks; a filter (kind, source, theme) plus ⇧X rejects everything shown. A 3-hour bin's 150 picks are not 150 decisions when 40 are the same scenery.
  • Compare takes. Picks the index clustered as takes of one thing (same clip, same place, minutes apart, similar telemetry shape) open side by side — Lightroom's Survey view — with P on one and the rest rejected together.
  • Overlap. Two keeps in one clip whose ranges overlap merge into one keep in the bin with both notes; a string-out never plays footage twice.
  • Undo. ⌘Z reverses the last verdict with its trim and note, across round boundaries, and after resume. The pass position, later pile and rejects are all in the EDL, so quitting mid-round costs nothing.
  • Re-index and added footage. Verdicts and notes are ranges on clip time. A finer look produces new picks; existing verdicts re-attach by overlap, and a pick that contradicts an old verdict is shown as such. Added clips arrive as their own round.

4.4 Dictation: the note that trains the next ask

The project's most durable finding is that a one-line human note is the lever. Notes only get written when writing is free, so the pass makes the note a push-to-talk gesture: hold V, say what you see, release. The clip's audio ducks while you hold, the same Whisper that transcribed the footage transcribes you on the GPU, and the text lands in the margin about a second after you let go, with N to edit it. The brief's names seed the recogniser.

Where it goes is the point. A note is a range comment on clip time — Frame.io's pattern — so it survives re-indexing and added footage, shows as a marker on B's events lane, rides into the bin with the machine's reason, and reaches the model per moment: "the editor said: hold on his face after." Later asks inherit it through the segment's why, the memory the revision loop already depends on. Keys stay authoritative — the note is annotation, P is the verdict; parsing intent from speech is a follow-up, not a launch feature. 1 marks a hero: it must appear in the first cut; the model may trim inside its range but never drop it without saying why.

§5Stage 6

The bin: selection as a first-class artifact, with a round trip

The bin lives in the EDL as selects beside segments: each keep is a clip-time range with the machine's reason, your note, its witnesses, its hero flag, and — after assembly — used_in, the segment ids it became. That last field is what the reviewer found missing: without it, a bin can't show what the film used, and the next ask from the bin can't know what you've already placed. So the bin has three filters, unused / used / rejected, a shot added by hand in B's cutting room becomes a keep automatically, and a keep whose footage went missing is flagged rather than dropped.

The first cut is asked from the bin: "build the film from these 14 moments, in this spirit" — the heroes fixed, the keeps' ranges as bounds the model may trim inside, the rest of the inventory available only for connective tissue. That is a different and better prompt than "here is 44 minutes, find the film." Two variants from one bin belong to the cutting room's Versions tab, which already exists in B.

§10Build order

Six steps, the pass before the door

  • 1 · The bin in the EDL. selects with clip-time ranges, reason, note, witnesses, hero, used_in; rejects and later as a floor list. Non-breaking; assemble.py ignores it. The first-cut prompt taught to read it.
  • 2 · The pass. P / X / U, keep-what-you-watched with outward snap, the two-tier strip, three-state witnesses, dictation with ducking, rounds with the closing card. This is where the time and the fun are; it can run today on the existing sidecars and events file.
  • 3 · The journal. Per-clip stages, priority scoring from the free passes, resume on restart, per-clip release, failure states. Turns the three existing buttons into one unattended index.
  • 4 · Added footage. Detect new stems against the journal, listen immediately, queue by priority, deliver as a round; relink by hash for moved files.
  • 5 · The open screen. Contact sheet with free flags, themes proposed from transcripts, the granularity slider, settings for workers and cap.
  • 6 · R11, then telemetry. Confirm GPMF in these files, extract, score against the adjudicated events and random windows; only then give the felt witness a weight and a vocabulary.
