Reframing the Cut Board TODAY PRINCIPLES A·REFIT B·CUTTING ROOM C·DIRECTOR'S CONSOLE COMPARE CALL
Roughcut · UI review · 2026-08-25 · no code changed

The board outgrew its page.

The cut board's bones are right — shots that carry their evidence, proposals you accept or discard, prices before spending — but its geometry is wrong: it lays an editing console out like a document. On the current Killington cut that document is 5,928 px tall, the monitor scrolls away, and five separate text boxes talk to the AI. This review measures the problem, states what a good AI-first editing UI needs, and pitches three directions — A a refit, B a monitor-first overhaul, C a conversation-first rethink — each with a render. My call: B, staged, stealing the best organ from each of the others.

  1. The monitor is a guest on its own board. 483 px of judging surface that scrolls away at ~600 px — the one component every decision depends on has no permanent home. revealMonitor() scrolls it back after the fact.
  2. The timeline is a stack of paragraphs. Cards average 241 px because every shot always shows its transcript, its seen-moments and its trim buttons — evidence you need for one shot at a time, paid for on all 21.
  3. The strip is the right idea, demoted. A true miniature timeline — width∝duration, colored by clip — but 36 px tall and click-to-play only. No trim, no drag, no zoom. It should be the protagonist.
  4. Eight panels, all always open. Ask, Find, Story, Music, Renders, library, keys — 1,997 px of permanently-expanded chrome for inputs used at very different frequencies.
  5. Five ways to talk to the AI. First-cut box, Ask panel, per-shot ✎, Find, Story — same act, different scopes, scattered surfaces with no shared history of what was asked and what changed.
  6. Judging renders happens in a 160 px window. Version comparison — the activity the whole project's method rests on — gets the smallest players on the page.

Load-bearing — keep at any cost

  • Shots carry their argument. The why, transcript lines, seen-moments, unusable warnings and polish provenance are the audit trail for a model that cannot see. No conventional NLE has this.
  • Proposals, never writes. Accept/Discard with a readable diff; the EDL on disk stays the single source of truth.
  • Prices and progress are honest. Cost on the button before spending; one top strip with real milestones; jobs survive reloads.
  • Posters, not streams. The connection-starvation lesson is paid for; any redesign keeps cards as stills.
  • Autosave + undo, and boundary warnings computed live.

Geometry problems — the redesign target

  • Vertical time. Length, trim and motion are spatial ideas; a vertical card list makes them arithmetic.
  • No permanent monitor, no source/program separation — Find's player, the monitor and the A/B players are three unrelated screens.
  • Input sprawl — five AI surfaces, eight panels, no session history.
  • No lanes. Music is a form; sfx/overlays (EFFECTS.md), markers and beat grids have nowhere to land.
  • Trim ergonomics: ±0.25 s buttons instead of dragging an edge that could snap to utterances the sidecars already know.
§2Principles

What a good editing UI does — and what an AI-first one adds

Fifty years of cutting rooms agree

  • Two permanent surfaces: the monitor and the timeline. Everything else — inspectors, bins, effects — is summonable and dismissible. Neither ever scrolls away.
  • Time runs horizontal. Duration is width; trimming is dragging an edge; moving is dragging a block; a swap is a drop. Direct manipulation with snapping beats any number of nudge buttons.
  • Source vs program. Material you're considering plays somewhere that isn't the film. Roughcut already has three accidental source monitors (Find's player, the library, the A/B renders) — they are one concept asking to be unified.
  • Keyboard first: J-K-L shuttle, in/out marks, ripple by default. The board's j/k/[/] keys are the right instinct.
  • An inspector, one consistent place where the selected thing's detail lives — instead of detail-everywhere.

New rules for an AI-first editor

  • The unit is the argued shot. Evidence must stay one gesture away from every clip — but on demand (inspector), not always-on (card sprawl).
  • One conversation, many scopes. Whole-cut ask (~$0.60 · minutes), shot ask (~$0.15 · seconds), find (~$0.14 · seconds) are the same act at different scope and price. One input with a scope control and the price visible beats five boxes.
  • Proposals are diffs, not chat. Review means seeing current vs proposed on the timeline and playing either — eventually accepting per-change, not only wholesale.
  • Provenance is trust. Which shots the model placed, which were hand-cut, which were modified after accept — visible state, like the ⚠ warnings already are.
  • Smart snapping is the AI dividend. The sidecars know every word boundary, onset and unusable stretch; a dragged edge that snaps to "the end of the sentence" is something Premiere cannot do. Manual editing here should be better than a traditional NLE, not a lesser copy of one.
  • High-leverage, low-frequency inputs (story, target, orientation) live in a drawer — set rarely, never spending permanent pixels.
§3Option A
A

The Refit

RISK LOWEFFORT ~1 SESSIONKEEPS THE DOM

Keep today's architecture — vertical shot list, right sidebar — and fix the four worst costs inside it. Nothing about the mental model changes; a person who knows the board today knows it after.

  • The monitor docks. Scroll past it and it shrinks to a corner picture-in-picture (~280 px) instead of leaving. It is never not on screen; revealMonitor() retires.
  • Cards get a density toggle. Default row is one line (~48 px): poster chip, clip, times, duration, warning ticks. The selected shot expands to today's full evidence card. 21 shots ≈ 1,200 px instead of 5,000.
  • The sidebar becomes four tabs — Cut (one unified ask/find input), Media (library + find results), Mix (music, later sfx), Out (renders) — with Story and project facts in a header drawer, and Keys behind ?. The sidebar always fits the viewport.
  • The strip earns drag and trim: reorder by dragging blocks, trim by dragging edges with utterance snapping, double-click to zoom.

What it buys

  • Page drops from ~5,900 px to ~1,600 px; the monitor is never lost.
  • One AI input with scope chips; sidebar always fits the viewport.
  • Ships incrementally — each of the four moves is its own commit, tests mostly survive.

What it doesn't

  • Still a vertical list wearing a horizontal strip as an accessory — two representations of the same cut, and editing splits across them.
  • No lanes: music, sfx, markers and beat grids still have nowhere to live.
  • The tabbed sidebar is throwaway against B — A-then-B rebuilds this work.
§4Option B — recommended
B

The Cutting Room

RISK MEDIUMEFFORT ~3–4 SESSIONS, STAGEABLEVIEW-LAYER ONLY — API & EDL UNCHANGED

A monitor-first console: everything on one screen, no page scroll at all. Two monitors up top — Source (what you're considering: find results, the library, past renders) and Program (the cut) — a single command bar between them, and below, the strip promoted to a real horizontal, multi-lane timeline. The evidence that today inflates every card moves into an inspector that shows it for the selection. This is the classic cutting-room geometry, but with the three AI dividends built in: edges snap to sentences, the command bar prices its scopes, and proposal review happens as a ghost lane on the timeline itself.

What it buys

  • Zero page scroll. Monitor, timeline, inspector and the AI input are all permanent; nothing important can leave the screen.
  • Manual editing becomes spatial — trim by dragging edges that snap to sentence ends and onsets, move by dragging, swap by dropping; the ±0.25 s buttons survive in the inspector for fine work.
  • One command bar with scope + price replaces five inputs; Find and the library become the Source monitor's tabs, which also gives version review a real screen.
  • Lanes make the roadmap cheap: sfx/overlay vocabulary, onset markers, beat-cutting, junk/orientation flags — every planned feature has an obvious home.
  • Nothing below the view layer changes. The EDL schema, every endpoint, the job system and all 15 modules of reasoning survive; this is an app.js/index.html rebuild.

Costs and risks

  • The browser test layer (22 tests) is written against today's DOM and gets rewritten with the layout.
  • A horizontal timeline needs real interaction code: drag, snap, zoom, minimap — the largest single piece of new JS in the project. Stageable (see the call, §7).
  • Small screens need care: the three-up top row wants ≥1200 px width; below that the inspector overlays.
  • Card lovers lose the all-cards-open reading view — worth keeping as a "read the cut" mode if it's missed.
§5Option C
C

The Director's Console

RISK HIGH — NOVELEFFORT ~4+ SESSIONSNEEDS BEAT STRUCTURE IN THE EDL

The radical reading of how this project actually works: on Killington, nearly every structural change came from an Ask, and hand edits were deletions and nudges. So make the conversation the primary instrument. The film becomes a storyboard of beats (cold open → the crew → the runs → the rocks → the button) — each beat a row of shots you can drag between beats or ask about as a unit. The right column is the session: a persistent, replayable log of asks, proposals, accepts and renders — the project memory that today evaporates into ~/work/app/asks/. Manual trim demotes to a popover on each shot; lanes and mixing defer to a separate view.

What it buys

  • Story structure becomes visible and manipulable — beats are how Karl already talks about the film ("the conquistador bit", "the rocks"), and beat-level asks ("tighten the middle act") map to how the model actually revises.
  • The session log is genuinely missing today: what was asked, what changed, what it cost, what was declined — currently unrecoverable without reading JSON. Provenance for free.
  • The EDL already carries an optional act field per segment — the data hook exists.

Costs and risks

  • Manual editing gets slower, and the brief explicitly says basic tools still matter: trim/move/swap through popovers is more clicks than either A or B.
  • Beats must be inferred or authored before the view works at all; a cut with no acts renders as one long beat.
  • Tracks/mix live in a second view — the multi-lane future gets harder, not easier.
  • Most novel = least proven; if the metaphor misses, the rebuild cost is B's without B's certainty.

§7The call

Pick B, build it in three stages, steal one organ each from A and C.

B is the only option where every requirement in the brief — basic tools that feel good, AI as the main instrument, inputs organized, tracks someday, no scrolling — lands on a surface that already wants it. It changes only the view layer: the EDL, all sixteen endpoints, the job system and the test-project fixtures survive untouched. And it doesn't have to ship at once:

  • B1 — the geometry. Dock the monitor, promote the strip to the full-width timeline with drag + snap-trim, demote cards to the inspector. This alone deletes ~4,700 px of scroll and is independently shippable.
  • B2 — the inputs. The command bar with scope chips and prices (A's one good idea, absorbed); Find, library and versions become the Source monitor's tabs.
  • B3 — the lanes + the log. Music lane with visible ducks, events/markers lane, and a History drawer — C's session log as a panel rather than a paradigm. Proposal-as-ghost-lane rides in here.

What I'd avoid: shipping A first (its tabbed sidebar is rebuilt two weeks later), and betting the board on C (its best ideas — beats and the session log — retrofit cleanly into B once B exists; the reverse is not true). Whichever letter you pick, the keep-list in §1 is non-negotiable: argued shots, proposals-not-writes, honest prices, posters-not-streams.

Per the brief, nothing was changed: the repo is untouched, no branches, no commits. Every number in §1 was measured on the live board this session; the renders are design targets, not screenshots.
